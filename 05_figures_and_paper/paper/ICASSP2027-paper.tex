\title{A Longitudinal Comparison of Supervised and Reinforcement-Learning Decision Paradigms for sEMG Hand-Gesture Recognition Under Intrapersonal Variation}

\author{Luis-Gabriel~Mac\'ias-Santill\'an, Lorena~Isabel~Barona~L\'opez, Marco~E.~Benalc\'azar, and \'Angel~Leonardo~Valdivieso~Caraguay
\thanks{The authors are with the Artificial Intelligence and Computer Vision Research Lab, Departamento de Inform\'atica y Ciencias de la Computaci\'on (DICC), Escuela Polit\'ecnica Nacional, Quito, 170143, Ecuador.}}

\markboth{IEEE Open Journal of Signal Processing}{Mac\'ias-Santill\'an \MakeLowercase{\textit{et al.}}: A Longitudinal Comparison of Decision Paradigms for sEMG Hand-Gesture Recognition Under Intrapersonal Variation}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Surface electromyography (sEMG) changes over time. Most hand-gesture-recognition (HGR) systems are calibrated once and never checked again. So it stays unclear if a stable system is truly robust, or just an untested guess. We report a six-month study of intrapersonal variation, meaning how one person's sEMG signal changes over time. Nineteen participants took part in seven sessions across six months. We ask two things. First, does a frozen deep representation shift over time? Second, does the decision paradigm, supervised or reinforcement-learning-style, change how a system reacts to that shift? We freeze a CNN-Transformer-encoder backbone (three sizes) after one calibration session. We then test a Softmax classifier and a LinUCB contextual bandit on six later sessions, with no further training after calibration. Two independent tests, Maximum Mean Discrepancy and a domain-classifier $\mathcal{A}$-distance, find a real shift in the frozen representation every month ($p<0.05$ after correction for multiple tests). This holds even though a classifier trying to tell months apart never exceeds 59.4\% accuracy, against a 50\% chance level. Accuracy itself falls only 4.6--5.9\% across all six backbone-paradigm combinations. This drop does not grow as backbone size shrinks by two orders of magnitude. Instead, a 7--9-percentage-point gap separates the largest and smallest backbones at every session. The representation shifts in a way accuracy alone does not show. The accuracy drop depends on backbone size, not decision paradigm. A stable accuracy curve alone is not enough proof that a longitudinal HGR system resists drift.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{urface} electromyography (sEMG) is a common way to sense hand gestures. It is used for prosthetic control, rehabilitation, and human-machine interaction \cite{Jaramillo2020,Benalcazar2017}. A hand-gesture-recognition (HGR) system ready for real use is still hard to build. One main reason is clear. sEMG signals are hard to predict. This is especially true in real time. sEMG signals vary between different people. They also vary within the same person over time \cite{Jaramillo2020}.

Researchers split this variation into two kinds \cite{Kyranou2018,Huang2017}. \emph{Interpersonal variation} is the difference between one person's sEMG signal and another person's signal. It comes from body build, body mass, fat level, sex, and age \cite{Ulysse2018,Unanyan2021}. \emph{Intrapersonal variation} is how one person's own signal changes over time. It comes from fatigue, mood, and electrode movement between sessions, among other things \cite{Schulte2022,Jiang2013,Zhai2017,He2015}. Most HGR studies deal with interpersonal variation. They train one model for many people. Or they train one model per person. Few studies look at intrapersonal variation directly. Doing so means recording the same people again and again over a long time. That is costly. So most HGR studies still test a model in just one session. Or they use short-term cross-validation \cite{Jaramillo2020}. This leaves an open question. Does a model that works well at calibration still work well later? Does it still work as intrapersonal variation builds up over weeks or months?

\textbf{This paper studies intrapersonal variation only.} We do this with a longitudinal design of seven recording sessions over six months, described in Section~\ref{sec:method}A. We do not study interpersonal variation here.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a growing area of machine learning. It has shown strong results in recent years, across many tasks \cite{Li2010LinUCB,Chu2011Regret}. Contextual bandits are a simple, well-studied form of RL. They have recently drawn growing interest for hand-gesture recognition itself \cite{LinPaskettYang2025}. Yet RL-style decision rules stay rare next to supervised classifiers in the HGR field \cite{VasconezV2022,VASCONEZ2023}. This raises a question. Does the same intrapersonal variation affect a supervised rule and an RL-style rule in different ways? Or is drift instead a property of the shared representation both rules read from?

This gives us two research questions, tested here with a formal longitudinal study:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Does a frozen deep representation shift as intrapersonal variation builds up over months?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does the choice of decision paradigm, supervised or RL-style, change how the system reacts to that shift?
\end{itemize}
RQ1 matters for a clear reason. A stable accuracy curve can hide a real shift. This can happen if the decision head compensates for the shift on its own. A system could then build up hidden drift. This could happen long before the drift shows up as an accuracy drop. RQ2 matters for a different reason. Testing two decision paradigms on the same frozen representation isolates the paradigm's own effect on robustness. Testing two different architectures instead would not do this. A change in architecture would only tell us about representation capacity. It would not tell us how a decision rule handles a shifting input.

We answer both questions with a six-month longitudinal design. A CNN-Transformer-encoder backbone is trained once, on session zero (Month~0). It is then frozen. Its output is tested directly for covariate shift, across six later monthly sessions. We use two independent statistical tests for this. We also score the frozen output separately with a Softmax head and with a LinUCB bandit. We repeat the whole comparison at three backbone sizes. These sizes differ by two orders of magnitude in parameter count. This checks one thing. It checks whether any pattern we find is just an artifact of one network size.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we give statistical evidence of a real shift. A frozen deep sEMG representation shifts across six months of intrapersonal variation. This shift stays mostly invisible in the accuracy curve. This directly answers RQ1. Second, we give a matched comparison. We compare a Softmax head against a LinUCB bandit on the same frozen representation, at three backbone sizes. We show the resulting accuracy drop depends on backbone size. It does not depend on decision paradigm. This directly answers RQ2. Together, these results argue for two things. A stable accuracy curve should not, by itself, count as proof that an HGR system resists intrapersonal drift. And comparing decision paradigms without also checking architecture size risks blaming the wrong variable for an effect.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the method. This includes the longitudinal protocol, signal preprocessing, backbone architecture, covariate-shift tests, and the two decision mechanisms. We give enough detail to reproduce the study exactly. This includes every hyperparameter, stopping rule, and statistical procedure used. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the covariate-shift results. This includes all twelve individual $p$-values and the correction procedure that checks them jointly. It also reports the accuracy comparison across paradigms and backbone sizes. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what these findings mean. It also states where they fall short. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes the paper.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview of the full pipeline. Raw sEMG goes through preprocessing. This turns it into a spectrogram. The spectrogram passes through the CNN-Inception stage. It then passes through the Transformer-encoder stage. This backbone is then frozen, at its final dropout layer. From there, two separate tracks run in parallel. Both run across the same seven monthly sessions. One track scores the frozen output with the Softmax head. The other track scores the same frozen output with the LinUCB bandit. \subsection{Longitudinal Protocol}
Nineteen participants took part in seven sessions over six months (Month~0 through Month~6). This design measures intrapersonal variation directly. It tracks the same 19 people across the full six-month span. Every model in this study is calibrated once, on Month~0. Each model is then evaluated frozen, on Month~1 through Month~6. There are no further parameter updates of any kind. This no-further-training rule matters. It lets us blame any later accuracy change on the passage of time itself. It rules out the model simply having kept learning from new data.

Within Month~0, we split repetitions 80/20 between training and validation. We do not split whole participants this way. We do this independently for each participant. This detail matters more than it looks. It guarantees something important. All 19 participants give data to both the training set and the Month~0(val) evaluation set. So all 19 also appear, unchanged, in every later month's evaluation set. As a result, only one thing changes between the Month~0(val) baseline and the Month~1--6 evaluations. That one thing is elapsed time. Which participants get tested never changes. Consider the alternative. If we had split by whole participant, some participants would sit entirely in training. Others would sit entirely in validation. Then any accuracy difference between Month~0(val) and later months could have two causes. It could come from real drift. Or it could come from a training/validation population mismatch. The accuracy numbers alone could not tell these two causes apart.

\subsection{Signal Preprocessing}
We normalize the amplitude of the eight-channel sEMG signal. We rectify it. We then pass it through a fifth-order digital low-pass Butterworth filter, with a 10~Hz cutoff. This gives us an envelope. The envelope tracks muscle activity. It drops the raw signal's fast oscillation. This is the same rectify-and-filter procedure used in prior work from our group on this dataset \cite{Isabel2023}. It also matches the standard model of the sEMG envelope as modulated Gaussian noise \cite{Shwedyk1977}. We split the envelope into overlapping 300-sample frames, with a 30-sample stride. We label each frame with a dual-tolerance rule, against the marked gesture interval. A frame counts as the gesture under either of two conditions. First, it covers at least 75\% of its own duration inside the marked interval. Second, it covers at least 50\% of the marked interval's full duration. We use whichever rule is easier to satisfy for that interval. This dual rule avoids a specific mistake. It stops us from mislabeling frames near the edge of short gesture intervals, where one fixed threshold would drop too many valid frames. We then convert each labeled frame into a spectrogram tensor, with the short-time Fourier transform. Each tensor is $13\times24\times8$ (frequency bins by time bins by channel). This is the same time-frequency method used for sEMG classification in \cite{stftTsai}. It is also the input the backbone in Section~\ref{sec:backbone} reads.

Class imbalance at the frame level is large. About two-thirds of all frames belong to the \emph{noGesture} class. A hand rests more than it moves during a recording session. We correct for this with balanced class weights. We compute these weights once, from the Month~0 training data. We then apply the same weights, without change, in three separate places. These are the backbone's training loss, the Softmax head's loss, and the LinUCB reward scale (Section~\ref{sec:decision}). Using the same weights everywhere is a deliberate choice. Suppose the two decision mechanisms used different class-weight rules instead. Then any accuracy gap between them could come from that difference alone. It would not come from the decision paradigm itself.

\subsection{Transformer-Encoder Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}
The feature extractor uses an Inception-style convolutional stage. Each block runs four parallel branches. Each branch picks up features at a different scale. The four outputs are then joined together \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}. This stage feeds into one Transformer-encoder block \cite{Vaswani2017}. We use this multi-branch design instead of a single-path CNN. We have a clear reason. Prior work from our own group tested this \cite{Isabel2023,HGREncoder}. It found the multi-branch design works better than a single-scale CNN, for pulling features out of sEMG spectrograms. A hand gesture makes a muscle-activity pattern. The useful time-frequency structure of that pattern is not fixed at one scale. So we pick up features at several scales at once, instead of using one fixed kernel size. This avoids tying the whole recognition task to one scale choice made in advance. Fig.~\ref{fig:inception} shows the exact structure of one Inception block. It has four branches. One is a $1\times1$ convolution. One is a $1\times1$-then-$3\times3$ branch. One is a $1\times1$-then-$5\times5$ branch. One is a max-pool-then-$1\times1$ branch. Each branch outputs $C$ channels. The four branches are joined channel-wise, into $4C$ channels. The $1\times1$ steps before the $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ branches use $R<C$ channels. They exist only to keep compute cost down. This follows the standard Inception design \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}. Without this step, a $5\times5$ convolution on a wide input would dominate the block's total compute. The large backbone stacks six such blocks. The medium and small backbones use fewer blocks. They also use narrower branches, given below.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_inception_block.jpg}
\caption{Internal structure of one Inception block \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}, matching the actual implementation. Four parallel branches extract features at different receptive-field sizes ($1\times1$, $3\times3$, $5\times5$, and pooled). Their outputs are concatenated channel-wise. The result passes to the next block, or to the Transformer stage.}
\label{fig:inception}
\end{figure}

After the last Inception block, we flatten the feature map. We project it linearly, to a $d_{model}$-dimensional embedding. We add a sinusoidal positional encoding to it. The result then passes through one Transformer-encoder block. This block has two parts: multi-head self-attention \cite{Vaswani2017}, then a two-layer feed-forward network. Each part has a residual connection and dropout ($p=0.4$). Attention-based models have beaten prior CNN and LSTM models at sEMG-based hand-gesture recognition \cite{Zabihi2022,Montazerin2022}. The reason is simple. Self-attention lets the model weigh every frame in the sequence against every other frame directly. This fits a gesture well. A gesture's onset and peak can fall at different points inside the same window. These points depend on the person. They do not sit at one fixed spot.

We train the full backbone end to end with cross-entropy. We use only Month~0 data for this. We use the Adam optimizer, with an initial learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$. The learning rate drops by a factor of 0.2 every 5 epochs. We use L2 regularization of 0.005. We use a gradient-clipping threshold of 1. We use a mini-batch size of 64. We train for up to 10 epochs. We stop early after 5 epochs without gain in Month~0 validation accuracy. All random steps use a fixed seed (\texttt{rng(9)}), for reproducibility. This covers weight setup and data shuffling. After training, we freeze the backbone at its final dropout layer. This sits right after the Transformer-encoder's feed-forward block. This freeze point gives us a fixed-length representation $\mathbf{z}$. Both decision mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:decision} share this same $\mathbf{z}$, unchanged. After this freeze point, neither Softmax nor LinUCB ever changes the backbone's weights. Neither gets a gradient signal back into it either. This backbone has a track record. At a larger size, trained on a much larger group, it reached 95.25\% accuracy on a 612-participant sEMG dataset, in prior work \cite{HGREncoder}. We cite that result for a reason. It shows this is a backbone with a real track record on this task. It is not an untested design made only for this study.

We train the same architecture at three sizes. They differ only in width and depth. This checks one thing. It checks whether any effect we report in Section~\ref{sec:results} is just an artifact of one network size. It should instead be a real property of the learned representation. Table~\ref{tab:capacities} lists the exact setup of each size, as built. The large backbone uses all six Inception blocks. It has 18 channels per branch, 16 reduction channels, $d_{model}=128$, and 8 attention heads. The medium backbone uses four blocks. It has 12 branch channels, 10 reduction channels, $d_{model}=64$, and 4 heads. The small backbone uses two blocks. It has 6 branch channels, 5 reduction channels, $d_{model}=32$, and 2 heads. All three sizes use the same feed-forward expansion ratio, $\times2$, inside the Transformer block. These three sizes span two orders of magnitude in parameter count. Every other training detail stays the same across all three sizes. This includes the loss function, the class-weight rule, the early-stopping rule, and the evaluation steps. So size is the only thing that changes between them.

\subsection{Covariate-Shift Testing on the Frozen Representation}
\label{sec:covshift}
Before we test accuracy, we test the representation itself. We ask a direct question. Does the frozen $\mathbf{z}$ sit in a different part of feature space in later months, than at calibration time? We need this test for a simple reason. A decision head can compensate for a real shift. It can keep accuracy flat while doing so. This can happen even while $\mathbf{z}$ has moved a lot. Only a direct test on $\mathbf{z}$ can tell these two cases apart.

We run two independent statistical tests. Each compares Month~0 against one of the six later months. Using two tests matters. It means one test alone cannot drive our conclusions. For each month $m$, we draw a random sample. We take up to 2000 frames from Month~0. We take up to 2000 frames from month $m$. We seed this draw as \texttt{rng(9+m)}. So each month gets a different sample. We can still reproduce every sample.

\textbf{Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD).} This test asks a simple question. Do two groups of points come from the same distribution, or not? It answers this with a comparison. It compares how similar points are within each group, against how similar points are across the two groups. If the groups come from the same source, these two kinds of similarity should match closely. If they come from different sources, within-group similarity should be higher than across-group similarity. Let $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_{n_A}\}$ be a sample from Month~0. Let $B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_{n_B}\}$ be a sample from month $m$. Both come from the 128-dimensional frozen representation. We measure similarity with a Gaussian RBF kernel, $k(x,y)=\exp(-\lVert x-y\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$. The unbiased MMD$^2$ estimator we use is
\begin{multline}
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^2 = \frac{1}{n_A(n_A-1)}\sum_{i\neq j} k(a_i,a_j) \\ + \frac{1}{n_B(n_B-1)}\sum_{i\neq j} k(b_i,b_j) - \frac{2}{n_A n_B}\sum_{i,j} k(a_i,b_j),
\label{eq:mmd}
\end{multline}
the standard unbiased form of this estimator \cite{Gretton2012MMD}. We set the kernel width $\sigma$ once per month, with the median-heuristic rule. This rule sets $\sigma$ to the median pairwise distance in a random sub-sample of the pooled data. The sub-sample holds at most 500 points. We reuse this same $\sigma$ for every permutation step below. This keeps the test fair.

\textbf{Permutation test.} We use this step to turn the raw MMD$^2$ number into a $p$-value. We do not assume any particular distribution for it. We pool $A$ and $B$ into one set. We then shuffle the group labels at random, many times. We recompute MMD$^2$ each time. This gives us a picture. It shows what MMD$^2$ looks like when group membership carries no real information. Under a true null hypothesis, the labels ``Month~0'' and ``month $m$'' could be swapped freely. We repeat this shuffle 2000 times. The $p$-value is the share of these 2000 shuffled values that are at least as large as our real, unshuffled MMD$^2$.

\textbf{Domain classifier and $\mathcal{A}$-distance.} This is our second, independent test. We train a classifier for it: logistic regression, 5-fold cross-validated. It guesses, from the frozen features alone, whether a sample comes from Month~0 or from month $m$. If the classifier cannot do better than a coin flip, the two months look the same to it. If it can tell them apart easily, the two months look different. We turn its cross-validated accuracy into the $\mathcal{A}$-distance,
\begin{equation}
d_{\mathcal{A}} = 2(1-2\epsilon),
\label{eq:adist}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is the classifier's error rate \cite{BenDavid2010Domain}. At chance level ($\epsilon=0.5$), $d_{\mathcal{A}}=0$. The two months look identical to the classifier. At perfect accuracy ($\epsilon=0$), $d_{\mathcal{A}}=2$. The two months are fully separable. We get a $p$-value for this test the same way as for MMD$^2$. We shuffle the month labels 200 times. We refit the classifier each time.

\textbf{Correcting for multiple tests.} This gives us twelve $p$-values in total. Each month gives one from MMD$^2$ and one from the domain classifier. Running twelve tests raises the risk of a false positive by chance alone. We correct for this with the Holm-Bonferroni method. We sort all twelve $p$-values from smallest to largest. We then check each one against a threshold. The threshold gets less strict as we move down the sorted list. This method has two properties. It is stricter on the smallest $p$-values than a flat correction would be. It also does not assume the twelve tests are independent of each other. They are not fully independent here. The same six Month~0 samples feed into both statistics.

\subsection{Decision Mechanisms: Softmax and LinUCB}
\label{sec:decision}
A Softmax head stands for the supervised paradigm. It takes $z$-score-normalized input. It uses a weighted cross-entropy loss. It stops training early, based on Month~0 validation accuracy. At every training step, it sees the correct gesture label directly. Its loss is computed against that label. In practice, it is one fully connected layer. This layer maps the $d_{model}$-dimensional frozen representation to six class scores. We train it with Adam. Settings: initial learning rate 0.01, L2 regularization 0.001, mini-batch size 128, up to 200 epochs, early stopping after 10 epochs without gain in Month~0 validation accuracy.

LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} stands for the RL paradigm instead. It treats the frozen representation $\mathbf{z}_t$, at time $t$, as context. It treats each of the six gesture classes as an arm to pick. It picks the arm with the highest score,
\begin{equation}
\hat y=\arg\max_i \; \hat\theta_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t+\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t},
\label{eq:linucb}
\end{equation}
a guessed reward for arm $i$, plus a bonus for uncertainty, scaled by $\alpha=0.5$. After picking an arm, LinUCB gets back only a scalar reward: $r_t=\pm w_{c^*}$. Here $w_{c^*}$ is the class weight of the true gesture $c^*$. The sign is positive if the picked arm matched the true gesture. Otherwise the sign is negative. Each arm $i$ keeps two things. It keeps a $d_{model}\times d_{model}$ matrix $A_i$, starting as the identity matrix. It keeps a $d_{model}$-dimensional vector $b_i$, starting at zero. From these, $\hat\theta_i=A_i^{-1}b_i$. After seeing $(\mathbf{x}_t,r_t)$ for the picked arm, we update $b_i \leftarrow b_i + r_t\mathbf{x}_t$. We update $A_i^{-1}$ in closed form, with the Sherman-Morrison identity,
\begin{equation}
A_i^{-1} \leftarrow A_i^{-1} - \frac{A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t}.
\label{eq:sherman-morrison}
\end{equation}
This update avoids inverting $A_i$ directly at every step. It keeps each update cost at $O(d_{model}^2)$. Ties in Eq.~\eqref{eq:linucb} happen at start-up, when all arms look the same. We break ties at random, among the tied arms. We do not always pick the lowest-indexed arm. That would build in a bias toward one class early on. LinUCB never sees the true label $c^*$ itself. It only sees this scalar reward, built from it. This is the key difference from Softmax. It is also why LinUCB is a real RL mechanism. It is not just a classifier wearing different notation. Under this update rule, LinUCB has sublinear regret guarantees \cite{Chu2011Regret}.

Both mechanisms warm up exactly once, on Month~0. Both use the same kind of rule. This is shuffled-epoch training, with early stopping on Month~0 validation accuracy. Both are then frozen for the rest of the study, just like the backbone itself. For LinUCB, warm-up runs for up to 20 epochs over the Month~0 training data. The data is shuffled each epoch. We check exploit-only validation accuracy after each epoch. We keep the best-scoring $(A_i^{-1},b_i)$ pair seen. Training stops early, after 5 epochs without gain. At test time, LinUCB drops the uncertainty term $\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:linucb}. This term exists to drive exploration, while a policy is still being learned. It serves no purpose once learning has stopped. So evaluation is pure exploitation: $\hat y = \arg\max_i \hat\theta_i^\top\mathbf{x}_t$. The two mechanisms' epoch limits differ in raw number. Softmax allows up to 200 epochs, with patience 10. LinUCB allows up to 20 epochs, with patience 5. This gap has a simple cause. A Sherman-Morrison update costs far less per epoch than backpropagation through the Softmax head. It is not a sign that one paradigm gets a more generous chance to converge. Both mechanisms stop under the exact same rule. Neither improves further in Month~0 validation accuracy, so both stop. Neither stops on a fixed schedule set in advance. This shared stopping rule matters, even with different epoch limits. It lets us blame any Month~1--6 difference between the two paradigms (Section~\ref{sec:results}) on the paradigm itself. It rules out one explanation. Neither paradigm was simply cut off too soon.

We now apply the Holm-Bonferroni method from Section~\ref{sec:covshift}, to these twelve $p$-values. We sort them from smallest to largest. The nine smallest clear their threshold easily. These are $p<10^{-3}$: all six domain-classifier tests, plus the Month~1, 2, and 3 MMD tests. The tenth-ranked test is MMD at Month~1 ($p=0.0005$). It must clear a threshold of $0.05/3=0.0167$. It does. The eleventh is MMD at Month~3 ($p=0.0035$). It must clear $0.05/2=0.025$. It does. The twelfth and last test is MMD at Month~4 ($p=0.034$). This is the single largest $p$-value in the whole set. It must clear $0.05/1=0.05$. It does. So we reject all twelve null hypotheses of no shift. Both MMD$^2$ and the domain-classifier $\mathcal{A}$-distance find covariate shift, at every one of the six months. This holds even at the strictest point of the correction. It does not hold only on average.

At the same time, the domain classifier's accuracy stays low. It ranges only from 52.8\% (Month~4) to 59.4\% (Month~5), against a 50\% chance level. This is an $\mathcal{A}$-distance no larger than 0.374, out of a max possible value of 2. This is our main covariate-shift finding. The shift is real, by both independent tests, at every month. But its size stays small, measured right on the representation. A classifier that is right only 53--59\% of the time cannot reliably tell two months apart. Fig.~\ref{fig:covshift} and Table~\ref{tab:pvalues} show something else too. Neither statistic moves in one steady direction across the six months. MMD$^2$ is highest at Month~5 (0.00467). It is lowest at Month~4 (0.00048). That is a gap of almost 10-fold. The $\mathcal{A}$-distance follows the same up-and-down pattern. Its own lowest and highest points also fall at Month~4 and Month~5. We come back to this pattern in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports Month~0(val)-to-Month~6 accuracy. It covers both Softmax and LinUCB, at all three backbone sizes. It also gives the relative drop, for each of the six backbone-paradigm combinations. Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl} plots the full seven-session path behind these two endpoint columns.

The drop stays inside a narrow 4.6--5.9\% band. This holds no matter the size or decision paradigm. Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl} shows both paradigms tracking each other closely, across all seven sessions and all three sizes. At every size, the Softmax and LinUCB curves mostly overlap. The gap between the large, medium, and small curves is far bigger than the gap between paradigms at any one size. Two points follow from this pattern. First, the 4.6--5.9\% band does not grow, even as backbone size shrinks by two orders of magnitude. So size governs the absolute accuracy level. Larger backbones reach higher accuracy at every session. But size does not govern how much either paradigm drifts from its own Month~0 baseline. Second, the drop stays about the same size either way. This holds whether the decision head sees the correct label directly, as with Softmax, or only a scalar reward, as with LinUCB. So the decision paradigm itself does not seem to set robustness to this shift. Whatever drives the drop acts before the paradigm-specific update rule kicks in. It most likely sits in the representation itself. This matches the direct covariate-shift evidence from Section~\ref{sec:results}A.

Every model has one single biggest step. It falls between Month~0(val) and Month~1, in both Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl}. It is not part of a slow month-by-month decline. Accuracy stays fairly steady from Month~1 through Month~6, compared to that first step. Two design facts about the protocol explain most of this one-time step. This is not a data flaw. First, Month~0(val) shares its recording session with Month~0(train). Both come from the same sitting. Both share the same electrode placement and the same body state. Every one of Month~1 through Month~6 is different. Each is a new session, with none of those things held fixed. Second, each model's stopping point is picked using Month~0(val) accuracy (\texttt{ValidationPatience}, Section~\ref{sec:decision}). This choice mechanically favors whichever split gets used for it. This is a built-in side effect of using validation accuracy for early stopping. It is not a flaw in one specific model. We could not fully separate two things within the drop percentages we report. One is this one-time session-identity effect. The other is real month-by-month drift. We flag this as an open question for the field. We do not split the two effects apart without direct evidence for it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken together, these results support a narrower claim. A reader might otherwise reach for one of two broad claims instead. One broad claim is that there is no drift at all. The other is that decision paradigm sets robustness to drift. Neither claim survives contact with both pieces of our evidence at once. A frozen sEMG representation shifts by a real, measurable, but small amount, across six months. All twelve tests are significant after correction (Table~\ref{tab:pvalues}). Yet domain-classifier accuracy never tops 59.4\%. This shift is only visible when we test the representation directly, as in Section~\ref{sec:results}A. It stays mostly hidden in the accuracy curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl}. That curve drops by only 4.6--5.9\%. It stays fairly flat across the same six months (Table~\ref{tab:results}). The accuracy decline itself depends on backbone size instead. There is a 7--9-percentage-point gap in Month~0 accuracy between the small and large backbones, with identical training data and setup (Table~\ref{tab:results}). It does not depend on whether the decision head is a supervised classifier or a bandit. At most, there is a 1.3-percentage-point gap in drop between Softmax and LinUCB, at any one size. This splits apart two claims. Longitudinal HGR studies often mix these two claims together, without saying so. A stable accuracy curve is good evidence that a system handles a given amount of drift well in practice. But it is not, by itself, proof that no drift happened in the representation the system depends on. A system can look robust by this common measure. At the same time, it can sit on a representation that has already shifted a fair amount from where it was calibrated.

This study has three limits. Each one points to a clear direction for future work. First, we test LinUCB frozen. It purely exploits, after its Month~0 warm-up. We set aside an always-online, classifier-guided version \cite{LinPaskettYang2025} on purpose. This keeps both paradigms under matched, non-adaptive conditions, for a fair test. Such a version could act differently under the exact drift we report here. It would keep getting reward signal through Month~1--6, instead of only during Month~0. Second, neither MMD$^2$ nor the accuracy drop moves in one steady direction across the six months. We do not have a clear mechanism behind this up-and-down pattern. Some factors are already tied to intrapersonal sEMG variation. These include muscle fatigue, sweat, and electrode movement \cite{Unanyan2021,He2015}. They could plausibly rise and fall from session to session in this way. But this dataset cannot settle the question on its own. It cannot tell whether these factors, some other physical or behavioral cause, or simply noise at this sample size explain the pattern. Third, we train the backbone once with cross-entropy. We then freeze it for the rest of the study. LinUCB never shapes the representation it reads from. So this study does not test end-to-end reinforcement learning. It tests only an RL-style decision rule, placed on top of a representation trained by other means.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We ran a six-month longitudinal study. It split apart two questions that get asked together too often. We answered each one with its own, separately checked result. First, does a frozen sEMG representation actually shift over time? Yes, and by a measurable amount. We used two independent tests for this. One is MMD$^2$, with a 2000-round permutation test. The other is a cross-validated domain classifier, turned into $\mathcal{A}$-distance. Both reject the idea of no shift, at every one of the six months. This holds even at the single strictest point of a twelve-test correction (Month~4, MMD, $p=0.034$, against a threshold of 0.05). The size of the effect, though, is small. The domain classifier never tops 59.4\% cross-validated accuracy, against a 50\% chance level. So the shift is clear in a statistical sense. It stays subtle in practice. Second, does decision paradigm change how a system reacts to that shift? No, not in any way we could detect. We spanned three backbone sizes, two orders of magnitude apart in parameter count (128 dimensions down to 32). Across all three, the Month~0-to-Month~6 accuracy drop stays inside a narrow 4.6--5.9\% band. This holds whether the decision head is a Softmax classifier told the correct label directly, or a LinUCB bandit told only a scalar reward. One thing does change the accuracy level, by a lot: backbone size itself. A 7--9-percentage-point gap separates the large and small backbones, at every session. This gap dwarfs the under-2-point gap between paradigms, at any one size.

Three clear next steps follow from the limits in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. One is to train the backbone end to end from LinUCB's reward signal alone. This would replace freezing it after supervised pretraining. It would test whether decision paradigm still fails to predict robustness, once the representation itself is shaped by that paradigm. Another is to test more decision-head families, beyond Softmax and LinUCB. This includes online, classifier-guided bandit versions, such as \cite{LinPaskettYang2025}. This would test whether our "size, not paradigm" finding holds beyond the two mechanisms we compared. Last, we could repeat this protocol on a larger and more varied group of people, than the 19 studied here. This would test one thing. Is the small, up-and-down shift we found a general property of sEMG drift? Or is it instead specific to this group and this recording setup?

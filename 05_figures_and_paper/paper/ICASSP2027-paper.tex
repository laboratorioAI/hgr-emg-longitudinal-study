\title{A Longitudinal Comparison of Supervised and Reinforcement-Learning Decision Paradigms for sEMG Hand-Gesture Recognition Under Intrapersonal Variation}

\author{Luis-Gabriel~Mac\'ias-Santill\'an, Lorena~Isabel~Barona~L\'opez, Marco~E.~Benalc\'azar, and \'Angel~Leonardo~Valdivieso~Caraguay
\thanks{The authors are with the Artificial Intelligence and Computer Vision Research Lab, Departamento de Inform\'atica y Ciencias de la Computaci\'on (DICC), Escuela Polit\'ecnica Nacional, Quito, 170143, Ecuador.}}

\markboth{IEEE Open Journal of Signal Processing}{Mac\'ias-Santill\'an \MakeLowercase{\textit{et al.}}: A Longitudinal Comparison of Decision Paradigms for sEMG Hand-Gesture Recognition Under Intrapersonal Variation}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Surface electromyography (sEMG) changes over time, yet most hand-gesture-recognition (HGR) systems are calibrated once and never checked again. So it stays unclear if a stable system is truly robust, or just an untested guess. We report a six-month study of intrapersonal variation, meaning how one person's sEMG signal changes over time. Nineteen participants took part in seven sessions across six months. We ask two things. First, does a frozen deep representation shift over time? Second, does the decision paradigm, supervised or reinforcement-learning-style, change how a system reacts to that shift? We freeze a CNN-Transformer-encoder backbone (three sizes) after one calibration session, then test a Softmax classifier and a LinUCB contextual bandit on six later sessions, with no further training after calibration. Two independent tests, Maximum Mean Discrepancy and a domain-classifier $\mathcal{A}$-distance, find a real shift in the frozen representation every month ($p<0.05$ after correction for multiple tests). This holds even though a classifier trying to tell months apart never exceeds 59.4\% accuracy, against a 50\% chance level. Accuracy itself falls only 4.6--5.9\% across all six backbone-paradigm combinations, and this drop does not grow as backbone size shrinks by two orders of magnitude. Instead, a 7--9-percentage-point gap separates the largest and smallest backbones at every session. The representation shifts in a way accuracy alone does not show. The accuracy drop depends on backbone size, not decision paradigm. A stable accuracy curve alone is not enough proof that a longitudinal HGR system resists drift.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{urface} electromyography (sEMG) is a common way to sense hand gestures. It is used for prosthetic control, rehabilitation, and human-machine interaction \cite{Jaramillo2020,Benalcazar2017}. A hand-gesture-recognition (HGR) system ready for real use is still hard to build, mainly because sEMG signals are hard to predict, especially in real time. sEMG signals vary between different people, and they also vary within the same person over time \cite{Jaramillo2020}.

Researchers split this variation into two kinds \cite{Kyranou2018,Huang2017}. \emph{Interpersonal variation} is the difference between one person's sEMG signal and another person's signal. It comes from body build, body mass, fat level, sex, and age \cite{Ulysse2018,Unanyan2021}. \emph{Intrapersonal variation} is how one person's own signal changes over time, from fatigue, mood, and electrode movement between sessions, among other things \cite{Schulte2022,Jiang2013,Zhai2017,He2015}. Most HGR studies deal with interpersonal variation. They train one model for many people, or one model per person. Few studies look at intrapersonal variation directly, since doing so means recording the same people again and again over a long time, which is costly. So most HGR studies still test a model in just one session, or with short-term cross-validation \cite{Jaramillo2020}. This leaves an open question. Does a model that works well at calibration still work well later, as intrapersonal variation builds up over weeks or months?

\textbf{This paper studies intrapersonal variation only.} We do this with a longitudinal design of seven recording sessions over six months, described in Section~\ref{sec:method}A. We do not study interpersonal variation here.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a growing area of machine learning. It has shown strong results in recent years, across many tasks \cite{Li2010LinUCB,Chu2011Regret}. Contextual bandits, a simple and well-studied form of RL, have recently drawn growing interest for hand-gesture recognition itself \cite{LinPaskettYang2025}. Yet RL-style decision rules stay rare next to supervised classifiers in the HGR field \cite{VasconezV2022,VASCONEZ2023}. This raises a question. Does the same intrapersonal variation affect a supervised rule and an RL-style rule in different ways? Or is drift instead a property of the shared representation both rules read from?

This gives us two research questions, tested here with a formal longitudinal study:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Does a frozen deep representation shift as intrapersonal variation builds up over months?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does the choice of decision paradigm, supervised or RL-style, change how the system reacts to that shift?
\end{itemize}
RQ1 matters because a stable accuracy curve can hide a real shift. This can happen if the decision head compensates for the shift on its own, letting a system build up hidden drift long before it shows up as an accuracy drop. RQ2 matters for a different reason. Testing two decision paradigms on the same frozen representation isolates the paradigm's own effect on robustness. Testing two different architectures instead would not do this, since a change in architecture would only tell us about representation capacity, not how a decision rule handles a shifting input.

We answer both questions with a six-month longitudinal design. A CNN-Transformer-encoder backbone is trained once, on session zero (Month~0), and then frozen. Its output is tested directly for covariate shift across six later monthly sessions, using two independent statistical tests. We also score the frozen output separately with a Softmax head and with a LinUCB bandit. We repeat the whole comparison at three backbone sizes, which differ by two orders of magnitude in parameter count. This checks whether any pattern we find is just an artifact of one network size.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we give statistical evidence that a frozen deep sEMG representation shifts across six months of intrapersonal variation, a shift that stays mostly invisible in the accuracy curve. This directly answers RQ1. Second, we give a matched comparison of a Softmax head against a LinUCB bandit on the same frozen representation, at three backbone sizes. We show the resulting accuracy drop depends on backbone size, not on decision paradigm. This directly answers RQ2. Together, these results argue for two things. A stable accuracy curve should not, by itself, count as proof that an HGR system resists intrapersonal drift. And comparing decision paradigms without also checking architecture size risks blaming the wrong variable for an effect.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the longitudinal protocol, signal preprocessing, backbone architecture, covariate-shift tests, and the two decision mechanisms, with enough detail to reproduce the study exactly, including every hyperparameter, stopping rule, and statistical procedure used. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the covariate-shift results, including all twelve individual $p$-values and the correction procedure that checks them jointly, plus the accuracy comparison across paradigms and backbone sizes. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what these findings mean and where they fall short. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes the paper.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview of the full pipeline. Raw sEMG goes through preprocessing into a spectrogram, which passes through the CNN-Inception stage and then the Transformer-encoder stage. This backbone is then frozen at its final dropout layer. From there, two separate tracks run in parallel, across the same seven monthly sessions. One track scores the frozen output with the Softmax head. The other track scores the same frozen output with the LinUCB bandit. Neither track updates any weight after Month~0.

\subsection{Longitudinal Protocol}
Nineteen participants took part in seven sessions over six months (Month~0 through Month~6). This design measures intrapersonal variation directly, since it tracks the same 19 people across the full six-month span. Every model in this study is calibrated once, on Month~0, then evaluated frozen on Month~1 through Month~6, with no further parameter updates of any kind. This no-further-training rule lets us blame any later accuracy change on the passage of time itself, and rules out the model simply having kept learning from new data.

Within Month~0, we split repetitions, not whole participants, 80/20 between training and validation, independently for each participant. This detail matters more than it looks. It guarantees that all 19 participants give data to both the training set and the Month~0(val) evaluation set, so all 19 also appear, unchanged, in every later month's evaluation set. As a result, the only thing that changes between the Month~0(val) baseline and the Month~1--6 evaluations is elapsed time. Which participants get tested never changes. Consider the alternative: if we had split by whole participant, some participants would sit entirely in training and others entirely in validation. Then any accuracy difference between Month~0(val) and later months could come from real drift, or from a training/validation population mismatch, and the accuracy numbers alone could not tell these two causes apart.

\subsection{Signal Preprocessing}
We normalize the amplitude of the eight-channel sEMG signal, rectify it, and pass it through a fifth-order digital low-pass Butterworth filter with a 10~Hz cutoff. This gives us an envelope that tracks muscle activity and drops the raw signal's fast oscillation. This is the same rectify-and-filter procedure used in prior work from our group on this dataset \cite{Isabel2023}, and it matches the standard model of the sEMG envelope as modulated Gaussian noise \cite{Shwedyk1977}. We split the envelope into overlapping 300-sample frames, with a 30-sample stride, and label each frame against the marked gesture interval with a dual-tolerance rule. A frame counts as the gesture if it covers at least 75\% of its own duration inside the marked interval, or at least 50\% of the marked interval's full duration, whichever is easier to satisfy for that interval. This dual rule avoids mislabeling frames near the edge of short gesture intervals, where one fixed threshold would drop too many valid frames. We then convert each labeled frame into a $13\times24\times8$ spectrogram tensor (frequency bins by time bins by channel) with the short-time Fourier transform. This is the same time-frequency method used for sEMG classification in \cite{stftTsai}, and it is also the input the backbone in Section~\ref{sec:backbone} reads.

Class imbalance at the frame level is large. About two-thirds of all frames belong to the \emph{noGesture} class, since a hand rests more than it moves during a recording session. We correct for this with balanced class weights, computed once from the Month~0 training data, then applied without change in three places: the backbone's training loss, the Softmax head's loss, and the LinUCB reward scale (Section~\ref{sec:decision}). Using the same weights everywhere is a deliberate choice. If the two decision mechanisms used different class-weight rules instead, any accuracy gap between them could come from that difference alone, not from the decision paradigm itself.

\subsection{Transformer-Encoder Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}
The feature extractor uses an Inception-style convolutional stage. Each block runs four parallel branches, each picking up features at a different scale, and the four outputs are then joined together \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}. This stage feeds into one Transformer-encoder block \cite{Vaswani2017}. We use this multi-branch design instead of a single-path CNN for a clear reason. Prior work from our own group found it works better than a single-scale CNN for pulling features out of sEMG spectrograms \cite{Isabel2023,HGREncoder}. A hand gesture makes a muscle-activity pattern whose useful time-frequency structure is not fixed at one scale, so picking up features at several scales at once, instead of using one fixed kernel size, avoids tying the whole recognition task to one scale choice made in advance. Fig.~\ref{fig:inception} shows the exact structure of one Inception block. It has four branches: a $1\times1$ convolution, a $1\times1$-then-$3\times3$ branch, a $1\times1$-then-$5\times5$ branch, and a max-pool-then-$1\times1$ branch. Each branch outputs $C$ channels, and the four branches are joined channel-wise into $4C$ channels. The $1\times1$ steps before the $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ branches use $R<C$ channels, and exist only to keep compute cost down, following the standard Inception design \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}. Without this step, a $5\times5$ convolution on a wide input would dominate the block's total compute. The large backbone stacks six such blocks. The medium and small backbones use fewer blocks and narrower branches, given below.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_inception_block.jpg}
\caption{Internal structure of one Inception block \cite{SzegedyGoogleNet}, matching the actual implementation. Four parallel branches extract features at different receptive-field sizes ($1\times1$, $3\times3$, $5\times5$, and pooled), and their outputs are concatenated channel-wise before passing to the next block or to the Transformer stage.}
\label{fig:inception}
\end{figure}

After the last Inception block, we flatten the feature map, project it linearly to a $d_{model}$-dimensional embedding, and add a sinusoidal positional encoding to it. The result then passes through one Transformer-encoder block: multi-head self-attention \cite{Vaswani2017} followed by a two-layer feed-forward network, each with a residual connection and dropout ($p=0.4$). Attention-based models have beaten prior CNN and LSTM models at sEMG-based hand-gesture recognition \cite{Zabihi2022,Montazerin2022}, because self-attention lets the model weigh every frame in the sequence against every other frame directly. This fits a gesture well, since its onset and peak can fall at different, person-dependent points inside the same window rather than at one fixed spot.

We train the full backbone end to end with cross-entropy, using only Month~0 data. We use the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$, which drops by a factor of 0.2 every 5 epochs. We use L2 regularization of 0.005, a gradient-clipping threshold of 1, and a mini-batch size of 64. We train for up to 10 epochs, with early stopping after 5 epochs without gain in Month~0 validation accuracy. All random steps (weight setup, data shuffling) use a fixed seed (\texttt{rng(9)}) for reproducibility. After training, we freeze the backbone at its final dropout layer, right after the Transformer-encoder's feed-forward block. This freeze point gives us a fixed-length representation $\mathbf{z}$, which both decision mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:decision} share unchanged. After this freeze point, neither Softmax nor LinUCB ever changes the backbone's weights, and neither gets a gradient signal back into it either. This backbone, at a larger size and trained on a much larger group, reached 95.25\% accuracy on a 612-participant sEMG dataset in prior work \cite{HGREncoder}. We cite that result to show this is a backbone with a real track record on this task, not an untested design made only for this study.

We train the same architecture at three sizes, differing only in width and depth. This checks whether any effect we report in Section~\ref{sec:results} is just an artifact of one network size, rather than a real property of the learned representation. Table~\ref{tab:capacities} lists the exact setup of each size, as built. The large backbone uses all six Inception blocks, with 18 channels per branch, 16 reduction channels, $d_{model}=128$, and 8 attention heads. The medium backbone uses four blocks, with 12 branch channels, 10 reduction channels, $d_{model}=64$, and 4 heads. The small backbone uses two blocks, with 6 branch channels, 5 reduction channels, $d_{model}=32$, and 2 heads. All three sizes use the same feed-forward expansion ratio of $\times2$ inside the Transformer block, and span two orders of magnitude in parameter count. Every other training detail, including the loss function, the class-weight rule, the early-stopping rule, and the evaluation steps, stays the same across all three sizes. So size is the only thing that changes between them.

\subsection{Covariate-Shift Testing on the Frozen Representation}
\label{sec:covshift}
Before we test accuracy, we test the representation itself. We ask whether the frozen $\mathbf{z}$ sits in a different part of feature space in later months than at calibration time. We need this test because a decision head can compensate for a real shift and keep accuracy flat, even while $\mathbf{z}$ has moved a lot. Only a direct test on $\mathbf{z}$ can tell these two cases apart.

We run two independent statistical tests between Month~0 and each of the six later months. Using two tests means one test alone cannot drive our conclusions. For each month $m$, we draw a random sample of up to 2000 frames from Month~0 and up to 2000 frames from month $m$, seeded as \texttt{rng(9+m)}, so each month gets a different sample that we can still reproduce.

\textbf{Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD).} This test asks a simple question: do two groups of points come from the same distribution, or not? It answers this by comparing how similar points are within each group against how similar points are across the two groups. If the groups come from the same source, these two kinds of similarity should match closely. If they come from different sources, within-group similarity should be higher than across-group similarity. Let $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_{n_A}\}$ be a sample from Month~0 and $B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_{n_B}\}$ be a sample from month $m$, both drawn from the 128-dimensional frozen representation. We measure similarity with a Gaussian RBF kernel, $k(x,y)=\exp(-\lVert x-y\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$. The unbiased MMD$^2$ estimator we use is
\begin{multline}
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^2 = \frac{1}{n_A(n_A-1)}\sum_{i\neq j} k(a_i,a_j) \\ + \frac{1}{n_B(n_B-1)}\sum_{i\neq j} k(b_i,b_j) - \frac{2}{n_A n_B}\sum_{i,j} k(a_i,b_j),
\label{eq:mmd}
\end{multline}
the standard unbiased form of this estimator \cite{Gretton2012MMD}. We set the kernel width $\sigma$ once per month with the median-heuristic rule, which sets $\sigma$ to the median pairwise distance in a random sub-sample (at most 500 points) of the pooled data. We reuse this same $\sigma$ for every permutation step below, so the test stays fair.

\textbf{Permutation test.} We use this step to turn the raw MMD$^2$ number into a $p$-value, without assuming any particular distribution for it. We pool $A$ and $B$ into one set, then shuffle the group labels at random, many times, and recompute MMD$^2$ each time. This gives us a picture of what MMD$^2$ looks like when group membership carries no real information, since under a true null hypothesis the labels ``Month~0'' and ``month $m$'' could be swapped freely. We repeat this shuffle 2000 times. The $p$-value is the share of these 2000 shuffled values that are at least as large as our real, unshuffled MMD$^2$.

\textbf{Domain classifier and $\mathcal{A}$-distance.} This is our second, independent test. We train a classifier (logistic regression, 5-fold cross-validated) to guess, from the frozen features alone, whether a sample comes from Month~0 or from month $m$. If the classifier cannot do better than a coin flip, the two months look the same to it. If it can tell them apart easily, the two months look different. We turn its cross-validated accuracy into the $\mathcal{A}$-distance,
\begin{equation}
d_{\mathcal{A}} = 2(1-2\epsilon),
\label{eq:adist}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is the classifier's error rate \cite{BenDavid2010Domain}. At chance level ($\epsilon=0.5$), $d_{\mathcal{A}}=0$, and the two months look identical to the classifier. At perfect accuracy ($\epsilon=0$), $d_{\mathcal{A}}=2$, and the two months are fully separable. We get a $p$-value for this test the same way as for MMD$^2$, shuffling the month labels 200 times and refitting the classifier each time.

\textbf{Correcting for multiple tests.} This gives us twelve $p$-values in total: one from MMD$^2$ and one from the domain classifier, for each of the six months. Running twelve tests raises the risk of a false positive by chance alone. We correct for this with the Holm-Bonferroni method. We sort all twelve $p$-values from smallest to largest, then check each one against a threshold that gets less strict as we move down the sorted list. This method is stricter on the smallest $p$-values than a flat correction would be, and it does not assume the twelve tests are independent of each other. They are not fully independent here, since the same six Month~0 samples feed into both statistics.

\subsection{Decision Mechanisms: Softmax and LinUCB}
\label{sec:decision}
A Softmax head stands for the supervised paradigm. It takes $z$-score-normalized input, uses a weighted cross-entropy loss, and stops training early based on Month~0 validation accuracy. At every training step, it sees the correct gesture label directly, and its loss is computed against that label. In practice, it is one fully connected layer, mapping the $d_{model}$-dimensional frozen representation to six class scores. We train it with Adam (initial learning rate 0.01, L2 regularization 0.001, mini-batch size 128, up to 200 epochs, early stopping after 10 epochs without gain in Month~0 validation accuracy).

LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} stands for the RL paradigm instead. It treats the frozen representation $\mathbf{z}_t$ at time $t$ as context, treats each of the six gesture classes as an arm to pick, and picks the arm with the highest score,
\begin{equation}
\hat y=\arg\max_i \; \hat\theta_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t+\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t},
\label{eq:linucb}
\end{equation}
a guessed reward for arm $i$ plus a bonus for uncertainty, scaled by $\alpha=0.5$. After picking an arm, LinUCB gets back only a scalar reward, $r_t=\pm w_{c^*}$, where $w_{c^*}$ is the class weight of the true gesture $c^*$. The sign is positive if the picked arm matched the true gesture, and negative otherwise. Each arm $i$ keeps a $d_{model}\times d_{model}$ matrix $A_i$ (starting as the identity matrix) and a $d_{model}$-dimensional vector $b_i$ (starting at zero), from which $\hat\theta_i=A_i^{-1}b_i$. After seeing $(\mathbf{x}_t,r_t)$ for the picked arm, we update $b_i \leftarrow b_i + r_t\mathbf{x}_t$, and update $A_i^{-1}$ in closed form with the Sherman-Morrison identity,
\begin{equation}
A_i^{-1} \leftarrow A_i^{-1} - \frac{A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t}.
\label{eq:sherman-morrison}
\end{equation}
This update avoids inverting $A_i$ directly at every step, keeping each update cost at $O(d_{model}^2)$. Ties in Eq.~\eqref{eq:linucb} happen at start-up, when all arms look the same. We break ties at random among the tied arms, instead of always picking the lowest-indexed arm, to avoid a built-in bias toward one class early on. LinUCB never sees the true label $c^*$ itself. It only sees this scalar reward built from it. This is the key difference from Softmax, and it is also why LinUCB is a real RL mechanism, not a classifier wearing different notation. Under this update rule, LinUCB has sublinear regret guarantees \cite{Chu2011Regret}.

Both mechanisms warm up exactly once, on Month~0, using the same kind of rule: shuffled-epoch training with early stopping on Month~0 validation accuracy. Both are then frozen for the rest of the study, just like the backbone itself. For LinUCB, warm-up runs for up to 20 epochs over the Month~0 training data, shuffled each epoch. We check exploit-only validation accuracy after each epoch, keep the best-scoring $(A_i^{-1},b_i)$ pair seen, and stop training early after 5 epochs without gain. At test time, LinUCB drops the uncertainty term $\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_t^\top A_i^{-1}\mathbf{x}_t}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:linucb}, since this term exists only to drive exploration while a policy is still being learned and serves no purpose once learning has stopped. So evaluation is pure exploitation, $\hat y = \arg\max_i \hat\theta_i^\top\mathbf{x}_t$. The two mechanisms' epoch limits differ in raw number. Softmax allows up to 200 epochs, with patience 10, while LinUCB allows up to 20 epochs, with patience 5. This gap reflects that a Sherman-Morrison update costs far less per epoch than backpropagation through the Softmax head, not that one paradigm gets a more generous chance to converge. Both mechanisms stop under the exact same rule: no further gain in Month~0 validation accuracy, never on a fixed schedule set in advance. This shared stopping rule, even with different epoch limits, is what lets us blame any Month~1--6 difference between the two paradigms (Section~\ref{sec:results}) on the paradigm itself, and rules out the explanation that one paradigm was simply cut off too soon.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Frozen Representation Shifts, With a Small Effect Size}

Table~\ref{tab:pvalues} lists the full set of numbers behind that figure, including the raw MMD$^2$ and $\mathcal{A}$-distance values, the domain classifier's accuracy, and both $p$-values for every month. A reader can check our Holm-Bonferroni correction directly from this table, rather than take it on trust.

We now apply the Holm-Bonferroni method from Section~\ref{sec:covshift} to these twelve $p$-values. We sort them from smallest to largest. The nine smallest ($p<10^{-3}$, all six domain-classifier tests plus the Month~1, 2, and 3 MMD tests) clear their threshold easily. The tenth-ranked test, MMD at Month~1 ($p=0.0005$), must clear a threshold of $0.05/3=0.0167$, and it does. The eleventh, MMD at Month~3 ($p=0.0035$), must clear $0.05/2=0.025$, and it does. The twelfth and last test, MMD at Month~4 ($p=0.034$), is the single largest $p$-value in the whole set, and it must clear $0.05/1=0.05$, which it does. So we reject all twelve null hypotheses of no shift. Both MMD$^2$ and the domain-classifier $\mathcal{A}$-distance find covariate shift at every one of the six months, even at the strictest point of the correction, not just on average.

At the same time, the domain classifier's accuracy stays low, ranging only from 52.8\% (Month~4) to 59.4\% (Month~5) against a 50\% chance level. This is an $\mathcal{A}$-distance no larger than 0.374, out of a max possible value of 2. This is our main covariate-shift finding: the shift is real by both independent tests, at every month, but its size, measured right on the representation, stays small. A classifier that is right only 53--59\% of the time is far from being able to reliably tell two months apart. Fig.~\ref{fig:covshift} and Table~\ref{tab:pvalues} also show that neither statistic moves in one steady direction across the six months. MMD$^2$ is highest at Month~5 (0.00467) and lowest at Month~4 (0.00048), a gap of almost 10-fold. The $\mathcal{A}$-distance follows the same up-and-down pattern, with its own lowest and highest points also falling at Month~4 and Month~5. We come back to this pattern in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports Month~0(val)-to-Month~6 accuracy for both Softmax and LinUCB, at all three backbone sizes, along with the relative drop for each of the six backbone-paradigm combinations. The drop stays inside a narrow 4.6--5.9\% band, no matter the size or decision paradigm. Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl} shows both paradigms tracking each other closely across all seven sessions and all three sizes, with the Softmax and LinUCB curves mostly overlapping at every size. The gap between the large, medium, and small curves is far bigger than the gap between paradigms at any one size. Two points follow from this pattern. First, the 4.6--5.9\% band does not grow even as backbone size shrinks by two orders of magnitude. So size governs the absolute accuracy level, and larger backbones reach higher accuracy at every session, but size does not govern how much either paradigm drifts from its own Month~0 baseline. Second, the drop stays about the same size whether the decision head sees the correct label directly, as with Softmax, or only a scalar reward, as with LinUCB. So the decision paradigm itself does not seem to set robustness to this shift. Whatever drives the drop acts before the paradigm-specific update rule kicks in, and most likely sits in the representation itself. This matches the direct covariate-shift evidence from Section~\ref{sec:results}A.

Every model's single biggest step, in both Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl}, falls between Month~0(val) and Month~1. It is not part of a slow month-by-month decline, and accuracy stays fairly steady from Month~1 through Month~6, compared to that first step. Two design facts about the protocol, not a data flaw, explain most of this one-time step. First, Month~0(val) shares its recording session with Month~0(train), so both come from the same sitting, the same electrode placement, and the same body state. Every one of Month~1 through Month~6, by contrast, is a new session, with none of those things held fixed. Second, each model's stopping point is picked using Month~0(val) accuracy (\texttt{ValidationPatience}, Section~\ref{sec:decision}). This choice mechanically favors whichever split gets used for it, a built-in side effect of using validation accuracy for early stopping, not a flaw in one specific model. We could not fully separate this one-time session-identity effect from real month-by-month drift within the drop percentages we report, and we flag this as an open question for the field rather than split the two effects apart without direct evidence for it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken together, these results support a narrower claim than either of two broad claims a reader might otherwise reach for. One broad claim is that there is no drift at all. The other is that decision paradigm sets robustness to drift. Neither claim survives contact with both pieces of our evidence at once. A frozen sEMG representation shifts by a real, measurable, but small amount across six months (Table~\ref{tab:pvalues}: all twelve tests significant after correction, yet domain-classifier accuracy never tops 59.4\%). This shift is only visible when we test the representation directly, as in Section~\ref{sec:results}A, and stays mostly hidden in the accuracy curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:sl_vs_rl}, which drops by only 4.6--5.9\% and stays fairly flat across the same six months (Table~\ref{tab:results}). The accuracy decline itself depends on backbone size instead (a 7--9-percentage-point gap in Month~0 accuracy between the small and large backbones, with identical training data and setup, Table~\ref{tab:results}). It does not depend on whether the decision head is a supervised classifier or a bandit (at most a 1.3-percentage-point gap in drop between Softmax and LinUCB at any one size). This splits apart two claims that longitudinal HGR studies often mix together without saying so. A stable accuracy curve is good evidence a system handles a given amount of drift well in practice, but it is not, by itself, proof that no drift happened in the representation the system depends on. A system can look robust by this common measure while still sitting on a representation that has already shifted a fair amount from where it was calibrated.

This study has three limits. Each one points to a clear direction for future work. First, we test LinUCB frozen and purely exploiting after its Month~0 warm-up. We set aside an always-online, classifier-guided version \cite{LinPaskettYang2025} on purpose, to keep both paradigms under matched, non-adaptive conditions for a fair test. Such a version could act differently under the exact drift we report here, since it would keep getting reward signal through Month~1--6 instead of only during Month~0. Second, neither MMD$^2$ nor the accuracy drop moves in one steady direction across the six months, and we do not have a clear mechanism behind this up-and-down pattern. Factors already tied to intrapersonal sEMG variation, such as muscle fatigue, sweat, and electrode movement \cite{Unanyan2021,He2015}, could plausibly rise and fall from session to session in this way, but this dataset cannot settle on its own whether these factors, some other physical or behavioral cause, or simply noise at this sample size explain the pattern. Third, we train the backbone once with cross-entropy and then freeze it for the rest of the study. LinUCB never shapes the representation it reads from, so this study does not test end-to-end reinforcement learning. It tests only an RL-style decision rule placed on top of a representation trained by other means.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We ran a six-month longitudinal study to split apart two questions that get asked together too often. We answered each one with its own, separately checked result. First, does a frozen sEMG representation actually shift over time? Yes, and by a measurable amount. We used two independent tests for this: MMD$^2$ with a 2000-round permutation test, and a cross-validated domain classifier turned into $\mathcal{A}$-distance. Both reject the idea of no shift at every one of the six months, even at the single strictest point of a twelve-test correction (Month~4, MMD, $p=0.034$ against a threshold of 0.05). The size of the effect, though, is small. The domain classifier never tops 59.4\% cross-validated accuracy against a 50\% chance level, so the shift is clear in a statistical sense but subtle in practice. Second, does decision paradigm change how a system reacts to that shift? No, not in any way we could detect. Across three backbone sizes spanning two orders of magnitude in parameter count (128 dimensions down to 32), the Month~0-to-Month~6 accuracy drop stays inside a narrow 4.6--5.9\% band, whether the decision head is a Softmax classifier told the correct label directly, or a LinUCB bandit told only a scalar reward. What does change the accuracy level, by a lot, is backbone size itself. A 7--9-percentage-point gap separates the large and small backbones at every session, dwarfing the under-2-point gap between paradigms at any one size.

Three clear next steps follow from the limits in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. One is to train the backbone end to end from LinUCB's reward signal alone, instead of freezing it after supervised pretraining. This would test whether decision paradigm still fails to predict robustness once the representation itself is shaped by that paradigm. Another is to test more decision-head families beyond Softmax and LinUCB, including online, classifier-guided bandit versions such as \cite{LinPaskettYang2025}, to see whether our "size, not paradigm" finding holds beyond the two mechanisms we compared. Last, repeating this protocol on a larger and more varied group of people than the 19 studied here would test whether the small, up-and-down shift we found is a general property of sEMG drift, or specific to this group and this recording setup.
